## Overview

This paper examines the legal status of autonomous weapons systems under international humanitarian law (IHL), with particular attention to the human control configurations that determine whether such systems can comply with the principles of distinction, proportionality, and precaution.

## Human Control Configurations

The analysis centers on three configurations:

**Human-in-the-loop (HITL):** A human operator approves each individual use of force. The system targets; the human authorizes. This configuration most clearly satisfies IHL's requirement for meaningful human judgment but limits operational tempo.

**Human-on-the-loop (HOTL):** The system acts autonomously but a human operator monitors and can intervene. The legal question is whether the ability to override constitutes sufficient human judgment, particularly when the system operates faster than human reaction time allows meaningful oversight.

**Human-out-of-the-loop (HOOTL):** Fully autonomous engagement. The legal status of this configuration under existing IHL is the central question the paper addresses.

## The Explainability Problem

IHL requires that targeting decisions satisfy proportionality — that anticipated civilian harm not be excessive in relation to expected military advantage. A targeting decision made by a system whose decision process cannot be explained or audited creates a structural problem: how does a commander verify proportionality compliance if the basis for the decision is opaque?

Explainable AI (XAI) requirements, as currently understood, do not fully resolve this. Post-hoc explanations of neural network decisions may be accurate in aggregate without being reliable for individual decisions — precisely the granularity IHL proportionality analysis requires.

## Culpability Frameworks

The paper examines three potential loci of responsibility for IHL violations by autonomous systems:

1. **The commander** who deployed the system — under existing command responsibility doctrine
2. **The developer** who designed the system — under a products liability-adjacent theory
3. **A "responsibility gap"** — the position that neither adequately captures the causal structure of autonomous system decision-making

The command responsibility analysis is most developed in existing law, but it strains the doctrine's requirement that the commander had reason to know of violations — when violations emerge from system behavior that wasn't predictable from specifications.
